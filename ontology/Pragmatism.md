Here we introduce a central idea in *modern* (serious) anarchist thinking, namely *pragmatism*. Anarchism is about affecting change in the moment wherever one lives in one's own conditions constructively for sum positive humanist outcomes. This is important because anarchism is often associated with direct action and in particular the use of violence against systemic oppression. The example of Gavrilo Princip, the anarchist who assassinated Franz Ferdinand in 1914 to light the fuse beginning World War I an example. Princip, a Serbian, carried out the act alongside Croats and Bosnian Muslims members of a group called Young Bosnia. Less than a century later however Yugoslavia whose government, now far more permissive and liberal than the failing Austro-Hungarian Empire, imploded with members of those same communities waging brutal war against each other. That said, the idea that the ends justify the means is frequently trotted out by so-called liberal democrats; usually when somebody else and their children are picking up the actual cost in blood and treasure; countless conflicts have been waged by so-called democracies since the Yugoslav Wars, many of whose outcomes or ends may be looked upon as anything but sum positive.

If the use of violence as a means for affecting change lost its practical significance with the use of the hydrogen bomb at the end of World War II, then  violent backlash following 9/11 laid it finally to rest. The use of force was categorically renounced by Japan following armistice while the occasional activist will set out onto the streets with with bricks and bombs to do damage to our commons. This however amounts to chaos and nihilism and has as much to do with anarchism as the Iraq War has to do with Jeffersonian democracy.

How does one then approach reality in the world as an Anarchist; that is to say someone who takes responsibility of one's own affairs and hold *only* themselves accountable for outcomes? Through pragmatism.

Pragmatism requires that we approach relationships with a *clear* sense of our relative position or bargaining strength as we seek to negotiate or transact in our self-interest with others to achieve *sum positive* results. Critically this requires the willingness to walk away from relationships we deem to be exploitative and which from our own perspective is no longer *attractive*. This is a departure from perspectives which celebrate self-sacrifice or martyrdom (usually someone else) such as those which espouse strict or maximal utilitarianism in the collective. Whereas it stands to reason that an instrument of great utility would first and foremost seek to its own maintenance. As we apply pragmatism, we invariably awaken to see optionality; and with that, a sense of gratitude and even optimism—goodness knows not for the parlous state of the world obviously, but for the clarity of the lens and our ability to navigate it

The defining flaw of modern liberal democracy is that outcomes are measured as a calculus of the greatest good for the greatest number (utilitarianism). That too as a *material* aggregate; which amounts to consumer-driven economic growth and an accumulation of things without *any* marginal productivity leading to the autonomy, leisure and wellness we all *actually* long for. It is the constant pursuit of happiness in another time without contentment here and now.
