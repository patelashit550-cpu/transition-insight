## The Primal Signal

Attraction can be understood as a holistic aspect of evolution: a signal that draws attention toward what may be transformed, refined, or brought into alignment. It is not merely a response to an external object, but a sensation arising within relationship — when curiosity is piqued by some quality that presents itself to awareness.

We notice, in a subject, artefact, or phenomenon, a quirk or pattern that stands out against the surrounding field of the ordinary. It appears as an outlier — something that signals significance before we can fully reason or articulate why. Children, especially very young ones, are wildly curious. Almost everything they encounter evokes astonishment. Adults find this delightful and generally encourage it. We smile as a child sits upright, eyes widening, seeking an answer from those charged with their care — someone they intuitively recognise as a trustee or guardian of both their immediate safety and their longer-term well-being.

This instinctive search for a guardian is the prototype for all subsequent authority. Leadership, therefore, amounts to mentoring, teaching, and guidance provided within the framework of a duty to care if systems are to generate consistently positive outcomes at both the micro (personal) and macro (systemic) level. Some matters admit of clear orthodoxy: boundaries that define appropriate and inappropriate behaviour. Others require discernment in context — the capacity to monitor and continuously adapt systems so that broad utility is preserved while innovation is integrated retrospectively as lessons are learned. Where leadership fails, it is often not for lack of process or policy, but for the substitution of genuine discernment and that duty of care or empathy, with procedural compliance. In such environments, integrity is reduced to performance, transparency to reporting, and trust to an illusion managed with bland boilerplate excuse and explanation rather than a confidence earned through consistent deliberate value-driven behaviour.

Our 21st century insistence on fairness demands something more exacting than this: an honest accounting of what is demanded of people in any form of community and why. Without this, systems drift toward a familiar pathology — they reward obedience over insight, repetition over imagination, and survival within the strictures of the organisational edifice over accountability for outcomes. Mid-tier leadership, in particular, becomes a choke point where attraction is systematically neutralised. Here, the standards of modern liberalism—pluralism, fairness and transparency—are seen as entirely theoretical, academic notions largely irrelevant to day-to-day administration. Curiosity and dissident opinion are tolerated only insofar as they do not disrupt established incentives; while creativity is framed as risk rather than opportunity unless it can be immediately instrumentalised—which is to say co-opted and exploited for naked profit.

Our objective, as parents, teachers, and indeed anyone responsible for the development of people within our purview, is to pay close attention to what those people are attracted to — and to understand why, when, how, and to what degree that attraction waxes and wanes. This attentiveness is diagnostic. What a person is drawn toward reveals where their creative energy wishes to flow. To ignore or suppress this in favour of institutional convenience is corrosive. The proper task of leadership is to foster and orient attraction so that creativity and imagination — the wellspring of innovation in every domain of life — are sustained rather than systematically exhausted. Without consistent application of these ideas, institutions begin to fail as instinctive repulsion to them develops in society as mistrust metastasises. Civic discourse then fails and so too ultimately, civility and civilisation itself.

One of the critical pivots modern society — indeed human civilisation — must now execute is the cultivation of a genuine sense of Calling in people, especially our young: an alignment between their personality and purpose that, collectively, generates positive outcomes for the community and the ecology. This imperative arises precisely as information technology begins to deliver on its long-promised capacity to decouple one's livelihood (or in more philosophical terms *liveness* or *anima*) from labour, ushering us toward a post-industrial reality in which employment, as currently defined, is increasingly optional. The difficulty is that many of the incumbent opportunities on offer to earn a livelihood are deeply unappealing — not because work itself lacks dignity, but because so much of what passes for work today is marked by a profound absence of meaning, purpose, and value for the community, as well as being unsustainable and extractive for the planet's ecosystem. In such conditions, the question is no longer how people can be motivated to comply or how their interpretation of reality managed, but how attraction might instead be oriented toward contributions—transactions, interactions, and behaviours—that are genuinely worthy of our commitment.

The antidote to this sclerosis is a radical return to human scale—a structural anarchism that privileges the small, autonomous unit over the sprawling hierarchy. We see the mechanics of this in the 'two-pizza teams' of our best functioning firms: groups small enough to maintain intimacy and trust, yet empowered with the sovereignty to own their outcomes for a well-defined stakeholder community or niche in the marketplace. We might debate the merits of the product, but it's the *process* that is the innovation in objective and performance-oriented governance. Even this works only in a narrow context. There is no ultimate panacea—only a dedication to observation and adjustment guided by the constant of attraction, all without the stickiness of irrelevant representations. In such proximity, work ceases to be a collision of pseudo-random personalities assembled by human resources, and becomes a genuine self-selected community. Within these bounds individuals bring their whole selves to the project without the need to self-censor to align with an overarching group-think, substituting the rigid compliance of the employee with the creative responsibility, fun, and mission of the guild.



## Set And Setting

But isn't attraction a prelude to the destructive consequences of impulsive behaviour? What about narcotics and other addictive substances? Or gambling? As well, what about the compulsion to preen and posture our presence through the accumulation of things; both the material (homes, cars, stainless steel appliances) and notional factors (celebrity, chief executive office, credentials, certifications or cash)? These questions won't be relevant to those entrusted with the care of the young; they have a natural (wild or untamed) aversion to bitterness or pungency, manufactured or otherwise. Creation signals attraction with the sweet, the savoury and the satiating (the satisfying sensation of natural food which keeps us from taking more than we want). As well—and I've seen this with my own children—the delight of being showered with many tantalising things at once will elicit wonderment, but as always, hedonistic adaptation and even boredom soon follows. One might as well have spent one's own time learning how to delight them with nothing at all except our imagination and a few materials with which to make crafts; or books read out aloud to them. Something which my partner and I learned and applied in the raising of our three daughters as they grew. I make this point to illustrate that the impact of winning the proverbial lottery materially isn't as gratifying as we imagine.

All of this is instructive. It shapes how we might use not only our money, but that truly scarce commodity — our time — which we so often must exchange for money in order to live within a complex economic reality. Much of the money held today sits beyond the satisfaction of basic needs — food, clothing, shelter — and therefore seeks purpose elsewhere. Beyond subsistence, that purpose is almost always cultural: meaning-making, expression, continuity, and shared identity.

While a thin slice of culture is enclosed and monetised through copyright to generate the majority of economic revenue, the *greater* body of culture — art, music, oral tradition, religious texts, folklore, literature, and even much technical knowledge in agriculture and engineering — resides in the public domain and is freely available. This includes works widely regarded as foundational: Homer, the Vedas, the Bible, the Qur’an, Greek and Roman history and tragedy, medieval literature, Shakespeare, Dante, Cervantes, most classical music, and much visual art prior to twentieth-century modernism, alongside the folklore and mythology of countless human traditions.

And yet, while capital accumulates in a relatively small number of accounts belonging to the wealthiest among us searching for returns, the majority of humanity remains compelled to trade their time simply to meet basic subsistence needs — often in work they would not freely choose if viable alternatives existed. Those returns are largely to be found in places that are irrelevant to those trying to meet the basic requirements for life for themselves or for non-adult children: healthcare (pharmacology including narcotics), controlled substances (alcohol, tobacco etc.), gaming (betting and lottery) and tourism. As well there are the 'industries' designed to cultivate a demand for these: advertising, marketing, public relations, data brokerage and surveillance—the last of which is also required to serve and protect those who have significant capital (money, material possessions and title to property) and who fear losing it.

In sum then, we have created in our world the set and setting for a host of amusements: coping mechanisms and *distractions*. Not attractions. These allocations of capital are not appropriate if we are to be public guardians and trustees to those in our care as participants in a holistic system of governance. Not to our children, who are surely the priority, and also not to those among us who as adults are susceptible to the temptation of these distractions in a world in which they are routinely touted ahead of meaningful product. Such application of idle capital looking for investment ultimately serves only to undermine the foundations of civilisation — families, neighbourhoods and communities.

In a system organised primarily around price, rather than value, the pursuit of return on capital becomes narrowly defined. Yield is measured almost exclusively in percentage terms, abstracted from context, consequence, or care. What matters is not what produces the return, nor why, but simply how much. Under such conditions, values-driven decision-making is treated as a sentimental preference for environmental and social governance (ESG) — an inefficiency at best, a liability at worst. Imperfect attempts to structure such investment opportunities in the fiat regime are derided, while institutions (university endowment programmes for example) who set out to closely monitor how they apply capital become mired in debate concerning their efficacy and impact because they (deliberately) look beyond the numerical return.



## An Occupation




>*I could talk all night* *My mind goes sleepwalking* *While I'm putting the world to right* *Call careers information* *Have you got yourself an occupation?*
>
>~ Elvis Costello & The Attractions (*Oliver's Army*)



The same logic is mirrored at the personal level where we arbitrage our time for money in search of an attractive occupation. Particularly in the developed world, many of us have had the luxury of acquiring a liberal education whose precise purpose was to cultivate the ability to discern meaning and purpose and come to grips with basic ideas of how both the natural world and the way we have modelled human existence within it function; and as well how one impacts the other to effect what we increasingly refer to as the Anthropocene. We call this epistemological and existential inquiry. While often derided as ivory tower concerns by those who occupy themselves with what were once called trades, they come to matter profoundly when systems begin to look compromised—when entropy appears to have set in. In real terms that is manifested as a world in which basic expectations of physical security and long-term health are undermined by seemingly random volatility or discontinuity (violence) and the increasing prevalence of 'modern' diseases which now undermine heretofore well-established long term trends of longevity and quality of life.

As we look out to that unfolding reality, and knowing full well that our lives are finite, it is only natural and healthy that we aim to choose vocations that will ameliorate circumstances and as it were imbue the prevailing truth of the world with beauty (order, structure and refinement) even if they do not return the best rate of return financially.

And yet, this aspiration is more fragile than we often admit. The capacity to discern meaning does not, on its own, confer the freedom to pursue it. For many, discernment sharpens not possibility but tension: the clearer one sees what matters, the more acutely one feels the cost of foregoing security in its pursuit. Attraction, under such conditions, does not disappear; it is postponed, bracketed, or quietly set aside in favour of what appears immediately necessary. The sensitive and introspective are often the first to recognise misalignment, but they are not immune to rent, tuition, healthcare, or the obligations of care that bind one life to others.

Thus a peculiar inversion takes hold. Those most capable of articulating value are frequently absorbed into roles whose justification rests almost entirely on compensation. Work becomes defensible not because it is meaningful, but because it is lucrative; not because it can be explained to a child in simple terms, but because it can be explained to oneself as prudent. “For now,” one says. “Just until.” In this way, financial return substitutes for purpose, functioning as a proxy measure of worth in a system that offers few others that are legible, portable, or rewarded.

What follows is not hypocrisy but accommodation. A tacit agreement is struck: one’s deeper attractions will be honoured later—after the mortgage, after the promotion, after the exit. In the meantime, the imagination is placed in reserve. This is how entire populations come to inhabit occupations they cannot meaningfully describe, except in the language of status, income, or endurance. The tragedy is not that people fail to sense what would give their lives coherence, but that the conditions under which they must live systematically train them to distrust that sense.

Time becomes our private currency, exchanged for wages that allow us to meet our needs within a system whose terms we did not set. Where work is experienced as alienated from meaning or purpose, the rational response is to minimise exposure: to reduce emotional, intellectual, and imaginative investment; to comply efficiently rather than commit deeply.

This is often framed as a healthy separation between “work” and “life.” In practice, it frequently results in the hollowing out of both. Work becomes something to endure rather than inhabit, while life is deferred — postponed until some imagined future moment when sufficient capital has been accumulated to permit contentment at last. Hence the familiar aspiration: escape from the rat race, retirement, leisure — a vision of fulfilment expressed largely in the idioms of consumer culture: travel, cruises, golf, distraction by other means.

What is striking in this imaginary is not what is promised, but what is absent. Rarely does it centre on social contribution, shared purpose, or deepened connection — pursuits that offer little in the way of conventional return on investment, yet which consistently correlate with human flourishing. Instead, fulfilment is outsourced to experiences purchased after the fact, once one’s most vital energies have already been spent.

This reveals a deeper error: the assumption that work is meaningful only insofar as it is compensated, and that meaning itself is something to be acquired later, once labour has ceased. In reality, work retains its dignity only so long as it remains aligned with growth — with learning, responsibility, and movement toward a purpose that the individual can recognise as their own. When that alignment collapses, remuneration alone cannot compensate for the loss. The return diminishes, no matter how attractive the salary appears.

A more holistic conception of work does not oppose labour to life, nor passion to responsibility. It recognises instead that mission, values, and temperament are not luxuries to be indulged after success, but the very conditions under which sustained contribution — and therefore any enduring return — becomes possible. The task, then, is not to flee work, but to reorient it: to discover the niche where one’s particular capacities meet a real need in the world, and where effort compounds rather than merely exhausts.



## The Trials of Job

To seek a new orientation is not to condemn the old. For many of us, the prevailing system—even with its transactional rigidities—provided a necessary scaffolding. It offered a venue for competence, a mechanism for security, and, for a time, a genuine sense of progress. There is dignity in the grind; there is satisfaction in the mastery of a trade, whether that trade is carpentry, code, or corporate strategy.

But there is a law of diminishing returns that applies to the soul as strictly as it does to economics. The dose makes the poison; to persist in such work is to overdose on the bitterest pill.

Eventually, the "lucky few" (and I am most certainly among them) reach a plateau where the correlation between effort and meaning collapses. We find ourselves, as it were, *grinding* at tasks we already know how to do—solving problems we have solved a dozen times before. The material rewards may remain, but the *vitality* evaporates. To continue past this point is to enter a state of arrested development, clinging to a script that no longer advances the plot. We are no longer the main character in our *own* narrative—and that is a profound problem.

This brings us to the archetype of Job. Often read merely as a parable of suffering, the story of Job is, at its heart, a crisis of transition as insight emerges. Job loses the structures that defined him—his wealth, his status, his predictable transactional relationship with reality. His friends urge him to return to the logic of the system: *admit you messed up, follow the rules, and get back on the treadmill.*

Job refuses. He holds out for a direct encounter with the source of things.

The lesson for us, standing "close enough to the end" to see the horizon, is not to curse the years we spent within the system, but to recognize when that season has closed. The trial is the terrifying, liberating act of releasing the wheel. It is the decision to stop trying to manicure a career and instead, as you say, simply *flow with whatever fate offers*—exercising free will not to accumulate more, but to be creative, productive, and present in ways that the ledger cannot capture.

In the end, Job is restored, but he is not the same man. He is no longer a manager of scope, resources and funding, but a witness to a whirlwind. He moves from calculation of triple constraints to an awe of a world which has the organic alignment to nature that would invoke it. That is the final, necessary shift: to lay down the tools of our competence and pick up the mantle of our curiosity.
