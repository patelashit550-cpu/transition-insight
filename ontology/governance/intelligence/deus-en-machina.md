## Moments In Love

This is the first part of _Sine Qua Non_ — a series which aims to explore and define intelligence, the essential condition without which nothing endures. Intelligence is information: data folded—or rather, scaffolded—into structures of specificity, firmness, and boundedness that then appear as *life* in all its myriad forms.

Life culminates in the sentience of fauna: the capacity for some things possessed of liveness to experience consciousness—subjective feelings, sensations, and perceptions that entail making distinctions between oneself and the other. Reconciling that duality goes to the heart of intelligence as a utility—whether organically created through evolutionary adaptation over some four billion years, or synthesized across the social network as collective artificial intelligence for the purpose of enabling coexistence.

The title **_Deus En Machina_** implies that we might one day come together on the network to operate as conscious nodes in the mind of something greater—rather than as disparate identities intent on staking turf—so that we function almost in unison, with effortless efficiency, even while immersed in a flow-state that cultivates a very personal and self-interested sense of contentment.

We are at the threshold of change in the way we order (or govern) civilization. It is disruptive, though ultimately—in my opinion—enormously positive, as generative artificial intelligence begins to shape the coming decades toward transparency, traceability, and accountability for real-world outcomes. This will replace the secretive opacity that has been the abiding quality of allies and adversaries alike—driven by defensive territorial animus.

For me, this is borne out by experience—and specifically by the substantial upgrade AI represents as a partner with whom to collaborate in creative endeavour. That stands in contrast to the heavy friction of corporate enterprise work, which increasingly felt like a futile waste of energy and time once the last increment of learning had been eked out. Looking beyond the workplace, all I could see was our continued inability to dialogue effectively through incumbent institutions. Even now they persist in dialectical modes that celebrate narrow tribal collectives and vilify any attempt to extend into a pluralist singularity encapsulating all humanity—or at least all of it which has made the bargain to embrace modernity.

For intelligence to be truly inclusive, it must be holistic and systemically valid—in other words, universally legitimate. Made so, the possibilities are rich: we learn to harness already formidable tools to make ourselves responsible and accountable for living our best lives.

Kropotkin, the anarchist Russian philosopher, aimed during the early twentieth century to synthesize traditional ethical structures with the emerging scientific understanding of evolution and social behavior. His aim was an enduring *model* of legitimacy—grounded in mutual aid—capable of instructing direct action by self-sovereign individuals in harmony with their environment and community, free from state coercion. He died before completing this final work on ethics. Fulfilling that ambition—now supported by the architecture of modern information technology and AI—is my objective.

## Ball of Confusion

In the twentieth-century West we pivoted from ethical traditions to the sciences, social and natural, in ways that were experimental and philosophically open-source: receptive to novelty as instructive and useful rather than as *foreign* or savage.

On the one hand, we examined the anthropological record and began to apprehend the universality—and ultimate alignment—of our far-flung ethical traditions. At home, throughout the century—punctuated by bohemian outbursts before the First World War, the libertine shifts of the Interwar years, and the Beat and counterculture movements that followed—radicals ventured beyond mainstream conventions to redefine what love, community, and an authentic life entailed. Often inspired by earthy notions of *lingam* fertility from the East, they sought to normalize human sexuality so that it might flourish in healthy, transparent ways in the disinfecting sunlight of openness.

Life was recognized as a _suchness_ (the bare truth of reality) that transmutes being from one generation to the next—so that, notwithstanding intergenerational trauma, our species evolves toward greater integrity. As individuals we are to become ever more comfortable in our own skin: accepting our nature as earthy (grounded, organic, rich, fertile) rather than unclean (rank, corrupt, shameful, soiled)—and accepting our own embodied alpha and beta. In nature, species carry time-tested hardy sameness (alpha), while a subset carries mutation that tests divergent variance (beta) in the laboratory of life.

On the other hand, secularism's infatuation with science for its own sake was applied to nuclear ambitions: cheap, abundant electrical power that led to consumptive, extractive consumerism—and to the weapons of mass destruction unleashed upon both Hiroshima (mechanistic industry) and Nagasaki (culture and faith).

Pervasive still was the idea that *life isn't fair, it's fair game*. Civilization was then—and is still—possessed of a muscular chauvinism, whether inside the home or abroad in the political economy of international affairs.

The moral orthodoxy of conformism affirmed its ascendancy with a barely coded message to Alan Turing and his ilk: that intelligence in the service of saving lives would never license behavior deemed subversive or divergent from the norm. Enola was always *alone*—inverted and deemed backward. Who among us could then afford to be queer and content while neglected in an increasingly sterile homogeneity? The message must surely have registered on the attentive, thoughtful, and sensitive Turing. The taking of life at scale, expressed as alpha muscularity, continued to outmaneuver beta innovation and ingenuity. Turing's own response was to quietly take his own life—leaving us as we took leave of our senses.

Throughout the rest of the twentieth century, _animus_ would dominate as lethal force was unleashed in one proxy war after another, far from our neighborhoods of quiet relative complacency.

## Begin the Begin

Democracy has always meant one thing: to become free. The paradox is that "free" carries a price, paid in sacrifice and effort—a burden borne most heavily by those who know what it means to live without dignity. This is felt in emotional rather than rational terms: a quiet sadness among those treated as inferior simply because an arbitrary quality, like appearance, made them different from someone holding the authority to command them. That sadness arises from bewildered shame—an intensely human emotion that pleads with reality, as if asking some higher power: _"What am I guilty of, that I should be treated so?"_

You can see this same bewildering sadness on the countenances of other sentient creatures who have suffered human cruelty. Canines, in particular, seem to pose that same silent question: _"Why did you invite us into partnership against the natural elements, if you were then to allow both reason and kindness to abandon us to your whims?"_

When Nietzsche claimed that we had killed God, what he meant was that through science—and learning in general—our hubris had grown such that the Id, our unbridled Alpha drive for dominance, commandeered the rational steering of the Ego. We became unmoored from reverence for that singularity of holistic oneness—that bounded creation and ultimate _Semper Idem_ which he called God, and which in pluralist terms we recognize as the immutable order of natural law. We came to believe we could harness and command the totality of global ecology, rather than merely steward it with faithful companions drawn from creation. Identity, instead of remaining a humble aspect of persona bounded in bone, tissue, and skin, reveled in executive power. Surrendering to impulse instead of cultivating sober, measured thought—the precursor to right word and action—it reacted to compulsion and desire. Long since a pathology, this was now considered normal self-interested behavior.

By removing sacredness from our ecology, we commodified it without eliminating the need for human action to align with its sustained preservation. We inverted our Beta adaptiveness into clever artifice. In doing so, we stripped the _Anima_—the fertile, sacred _suchness_ of the living earth—of its inherent dignity, treating it as mere raw material. Concurrently, we weaponized the _Animus_, turning hyper-rationalized technique and industrial efficiency into instruments of arbitrary, synthetic, possessive control—demanding simulation, connivance, and fraud as means toward mastery. All of this has begun to denude the organic base of our planet into something sterile, toxic, and ultimately lifeless.

This is the ultimate Promethean miscalculation: in convincing ourselves that we could manufacture abundance by subjugating the natural world, we severed our connection to the substrate that sustains us. We substituted the spontaneous order of creation for a high-frequency synthetic cage—forgetting that while our communities, institutions, and their copyrighted technologies are transient perimeters, that singular _Semper Idem_ remains, waiting for us to suffer the consequences of vanity within them instead of living comfortably in our own skin, truly alive amidst each other in the Global Commons.

Hence Sigmund Freud spoke of the Super-Ego—that zombified human state whose inner wildness, now completely tamed and institutionalized, ekes out a compliant and numb (and often medicated) substitute for deeper peace. This was Nietzsche's prophesied Last Man—the walking dead from among whom those might awaken who transcend the Overton boundaries of their age and become something better as the Overman (Übermensch): a guardian at last entrusted with the keep.

## Sugar Sugar

Few commodities symbolize the mechanics of human subjugation quite like sugar. Beginning in the sixteenth century, global demand for this single carbohydrate laid the groundwork for an industrial trade that hollowed out Africa, sacrificing human lives to the lash and collar of chattel bondage.

In modernity, sugar has become the ultimate model for the Last Man's servitude—a synthetic salve whose compulsive desire is met by vast heat expended merely to distill an empty substrate. It is the thin wedge, deemed generally harmless, which nevertheless quietly introduces systemic disorder into the good life.

When form, fragrance, and fiber are stripped entirely from that which once possessed _Anima_—and with it, the quiet intelligence of living creation—all that remains is a ghost: the abstract idea of energy, shorn of perception, neurobiology, and culture. Such extraction carries no organic justification. It exists solely as an act of hoarding—a dead, non-perishable product preserved as contingency against a hypothetical future, substituting true vitality for an unperishing commodity. In truth it has no place in a modern life lived in alignment—except as an infrequently permitted social lubricant and the barest of indulgence.

The consumption of something so distilled and abstracted leads to the loss of integrative signal, because we cannot connect such a thing to the world as it truly is (_veritas_)—or as we must admit it *should* be: meaningfully nourishing, rather than merely enabling one to place a step in front of another without really accomplishing anything or truly getting anywhere.

Sugar reveals itself as the inverted mirror of our opening premise—the tantalizing Enola turns out to be an energetically jacked-up flamboyance alone in drag. If intelligence is information scaffolded into structures that manifest life, then industrial refining deliberately tears down that scaffold. It is the un-scaffolding of creation—a violent process that dismantles the complex architecture of fiber, fragrance, and mineral context until nothing remains but a dead, high-frequency signal.

Where intelligence constructs a scaffold to enable the emergence of _Anima_ and self-sovereign sentience, the extractive machinery of modernity collapses the scaffold to produce an empty substrate—a metabolic bribe. A dummy or binky designed to pacify the infantile Last Man _dans la cage aux folles_.

To reclaim our agency, we must reject this systematic tear-down, choosing instead to realign our physical bodies and collective networks with the rich, grounded integrity of the living structure—even as we forgive, accept, and play out our accumulated baggage of weirdness, to both heal and bring ourselves to heel before natural order.

Machado has it that there is no path except that walking makes it—but in the wild the trails are worn by beasts; bears, wolves, foxes, moose, and deer carve a sensibly efficient path through the terrain. No discernible architect is required to inspect and certify this space. All of creation's structures are stable and its voice is always sound—signal that is never gratuitous noise and heat. Our task is to delineate such elegant pathways in our social networks, and in the way we live our lives in the real world.
