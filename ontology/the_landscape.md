How did we come to be here and where are we going? Let's first talk about projects. Inside of the opaque black box called the corporation the project was for a long time the mechanism through which that institution evolved as it adapted to a changing marketplace as it monitored its performance - a metric of how well it resonated and engaged with people. It was the basis for the work being done in various internal and client-facing departments. A key characteristic of the project was that it fulfilled a transformation of states (before and after) within a finite time period: it had a definite start date and a definite end date both marked by significant ceremony. The project has come to be superseded by the Product which amounts to a project with no discernible end: it gets off the ground as a project then faces the marketplace as an product (a need met as a good or service offline or online). It persists so long as it generates a yield; namely a surplus which exceeds the cost of creating and sustaining the product. The yield is a bi-directional transaction. As one thing is provided by a user so to is due consideration is passed in the other direction by that application which is in use. The thing provided may only be information that proves a sum positive change for the counterparties. It may only be that you are breathing evenly in a state of restful calm as basic due consideration to receive a bare basic income from the sovereign monetary authority (for example the Bank of Canada via your bank). After that anything you do is up to you to generate whatever value is meaningful to you not necessarily someone else.

## Come Together

The long arc of human history leans from the central trusted authority of hegemony and its often necessarily coercive tendencies towards personal agency and autonomy. We're completing a macro version of The Hero's Journey as articulated by Joseph Campbell in *The Hero of with a Thousand Faces* (1949) by coming together full circle and returning to modern version of a distant past in which we lived in relative leisure and comfort in the midst of community. Prior to the emergence of large states we existed in small closely knit tribal communities in which the exchange of value didn't need to be recorded precisely because for all practical purposes we were kin and our emotive and empathic attachment to one another superseded anything material we might have to offer. In a few isolated parts of the world people we still live like this; this change is driven by innovations that make transparent at a global level opportunities for the most productive allocation of our collective accumulated capital: Opportunities which align the interests of the individual to their community and to the *global* commons; they generally begin with attending to basic needs of *developing* communities (nutrition, education and base infrastructure such as sanitation and acute medical care).

Of course it's won't be *exactly* like the distant past however we will have the option of living in such pastoral settings and with considerably more personal comfort and security. More likely we will be members of a variety of *contextual* communities. These nodes will form as develop logical proximity on the network to those whose qualities are at least complementary or even in profound alignment to our own as we express *calling* based on personal attributes and interests. 

Metcalfe's Law is the notion that networks have value to us. There is strength in numbers goes the old saw and that ultimate form of comfort is safety and security of person. Once this has been provisioned we begin to appreciate other aspects of comfort such as the respite and leisure which comes from a division of labour and the rationalization of work. Even the isolated herdsmen and farmer will adhere to a larger community, not just for security but also for a cultural breadth providing a vast breadth of intellectual stimulation and the emotional succour.

The law also applies to our modern information technology and networks. As a second year student at Western, my IBM 286 clone running WordStar was a vast improvement over my Olympus electric typewriter. But that same computer, connected to a network terminal at Carleton in 1993, became something else entirely — on returning from my exchange term at the International University of Japan I could reach my girlfriend still in Tokyo, and later when she returned to her home in France, at no cost, at a time when international fixed line calls were still prohibitively expensive and the cellular phone was years from being commonplace. By 1995 the early web had taken shape and during my third stint in Japan I felt nowhere near as isolated as in 1991, when staying in touch meant writing letters and negotiating the best moment to call across a twelve-hour time difference. But even in Tokyo in 1995 the cell phone was nothing like ubiquitous. Living in Kunitachi and staying in touch with your girl in Roppongi was a real task requiring coordination, thought and planning — and I got rung out plenty of times for not making the obligatory follow-up call sooner. Relationships were barely getting off the ground before being abruptly cut-off (much like the phone call itself). That said when you managed to pull it off and gather a group to spend time it was really something special and you dwelled over the occasion - communing for the entire day or even a weekend.

## News Of  The  World

Fast forward to the present: notifications, messages and email arrive in a torrent we can't stem, and we stay glued to our devices for fear of missing precisely that tidal wave that is overwhelming us. The boundaries between home and work have dissolved, our relationships grown increasingly atomistic and transactional, and beneath it all runs a current of profound alienation and angst. Rates of addiction and suicide climb as we struggle with what has become a genuinely dystopian present — one in which we feel predated upon not only by outright fraudsters but by insecure acquaintances shilling their version of the good life on social media, while the gap between that performative luxury and the reality most people are living grows wider by the year.

In this zero-trust age, much as in previous eras when people first congregated in vast anonymous systems, we cluster instinctively toward the strong for safety. We begin to behave like particles in a three-body problem — drawn inexorably to mass, indifferent to questions of identity or character. Employment at an established institution feels not merely safe but prestigious. We accumulate credentials and certifications, curry favour with trusted intermediaries, and outsource our credibility to the systemically important financial institutions and media companies that have made themselves the utility layer of public life.

This is the Matthew Effect in its modern form. Like the feudal systems of earlier centuries, it extracts a toll in terms of autonomy and agency in exchange for safety and an affectation of arbitrary identity. One's nationality and or currency - the (Swiss) franc, dollar or deutschmark have become sources of prestige. These are  arbitrary because they are a matter of accident; where one is born or raised is hardly ever a matter of deliberate agency or choice (never mind that the extraordinarily good fortune is that you are born at all). The brands we cluster around cultivate loyalty assiduously — with the careful digital management of brand identity. And yet these brands draw a firm line between their immediate stakeholders (shareholders and employees) and everyone else by defining a semantic perimeter or boundary. One is favoured over the other and an extractive system develops to harvest a techno-feudal rate of return. Such is the power of this effect that over time influence and capability accumulate such that seven of the five hundred firms constituting the dominant passive index the S&P 500 account for over thirty percent of so-called value. None of these companies even existed when I was born.

The firm has one obligation: return to shareholders. Any deviation is a misappropriation of capital.

The mechanism for this tenet of doctrinal faith is provided in Jensen and Meckling's 1976 paper — *Theory of the Firm* — supplied the mechanism. When ownership and management separate, you create a principal-agent problem which has to be negotiated by the owners (shareholders) represented by a Board of Directors who incentivize senior managers, most of all the principal (the headmaster at the apex of the hierarchy) with pay and perquisites (in particular stocks options) to deliver the results shareholders want - capital gains - a continuous increase in the value of the shares, even as the vast majority of shareholders are now passive owners rapidly buying and selling stock and even borrowing stock for the express purpose of selling it to earn a return.

On its own there's nothing wrong with this necessarily but given the considerable opacity we can expect that a wide income and wealth inequality gap opens up between upper management and their various administrators across the departments (Human Resources, Marketing, Finance and Operation) beyond those in the engineering teams (Information Technology) who do the actual creative work. # The Long Education

## Act I — IBM: Learning the Machine (1999 – 2010)

For over a decade the work was IBM — and IBM meant everything large, everything complex, everything consequential. Air Canada. Bell Canada. Scotiabank. Manulife. TELUS. The institutions that moved capital, connected people, held records. The clients were not companies so much as systems — vast, slow-moving, deeply entangled with the society they served.

The entry point was technical. Systems analysis. Application development. Then the work shifted toward the harder problem of how organisations actually function under pressure. Project control, project management, program delivery. The titles changed but the question remained constant: how does a complex system move from one state to another without breaking itself?

The answer, learned across multi-million dollar programs and teams of forty, ninety, a hundred people, is that it rarely does so cleanly. A decade at IBM was learning to hold the tension — between what institutions say they want and what they can absorb; between the elegant solution and the one that survives contact with the organisation.

---

## Act II — Transition Insight: The Pattern Emerges (2010 – Present)

When the work is yours to bring rather than assigned, the vantage point shifts. The consulting years became a traversal — RBC, TD, Loblaw, the Greater Toronto Airports Authority, BMO, Transamerica. Financial services. Retail. Infrastructure. Insurance. Different organisations, different cultures, different pressures. Underneath the variation, the same structural grammar repeating.

Every troubled project shared the same anatomy. The gap between the people who owned the problem and the people solving it. Technical debt accumulated not from incompetence but from the pressure of the present crowding out the future. The roadmap that existed but had never been believed.

The work was to enter that gap and make it traversable — installing the scaffolding not as ideology but as instruments for making the invisible visible. To establish, in organisation after organisation, the basic precondition of delivery: that everyone in the room knows what they are actually trying to do.

A pattern accumulated that no single engagement could have revealed. Institutions are extraordinarily good at processing known work and extraordinarily bad at processing change. The larger the institution, the more this is true.

---

## Act III — Thomson Reuters: Synthesis at Scale (2019 – 2022)

One hundred people. Five teams. A codebase carrying years of accumulated decisions, all of them now structural. The mandate was not simply to deliver features but to transform how a large organisation understands its own capacity to deliver.

Core Platform, API Governance, Data Engine, User Admin — each team with its own rhythm, its own indicators, its own accountability. Velocity, carryover, work-in-progress, technical debt as a managed liability rather than invisible drag. Over two years, operating expenses reduced by one and a half million dollars — not through cuts, but through the elimination of friction.

The limit was also clarified. The system could be made legible, efficient, self-aware. But its purpose was given. The question of what the system was for was not on the table.
