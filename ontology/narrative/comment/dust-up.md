## War by Other Means

Prime Minister Mark Carney recently admitted to assessing war scenarios with the United States. This would have been unthinkable five or ten years ago, but in the current atmosphere, these are worthwhile thought exercises. They force us to think hard about what war actually is: an attempt to accumulate wealth—required for the exercise of influence, power, and authority—so that a state may do as it wishes.

The use of military force by the United States against Canada remains unimaginable. For the nation that used atomic weapons to turn, nearly a century later, upon its peaceable neighbor—the first country to unilaterally renounce the weaponization of nuclear technology after World War II—would be nothing short of an utter calamity for the human experiment.

With outright war unthinkable, the Trump Administration has sought other means to exert influence over Canada. U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent called an independent Alberta a natural, energy-rich partner for the United States, suggesting the province could potentially join the Union. That remark is one of many tactical forays across social media channels designed to foment distrust and disunity in Canada. And it is working: an Alberta man was recently charged with issuing open death threats against the Prime Minister.

This is war carried out by other means: not through armored divisions across the 49th parallel, but through the deliberate cultivation of social animus, regional resentment, and digital extraction. When hard geopolitical conquest becomes cost-prohibitive, statecraft degenerates into a low-intensity campaign where everyone seeks to shade their neighbors on social networks or extract monetary advantage from domestic fractures. Physical annexation is rendered obsolete when a sovereign neighbor's social cohesion can be dismantled from a keyboard.

Notwithstanding these occasional outbursts, the administration's primary weapon is an aggressively protectionist trade policy—rhetorically, at least. Protectionism has long been Donald Trump's refrain; an earlier iteration of the man on _The Oprah Winfrey Show_ in the 1980s spoke of the hollowing out of America's industrial heartland and the need to protect good, well-paying jobs for ordinary people. Some forty years have passed since then, and the world is a profoundly different place.

### Attention!

Our social network’s true currency—the only resource each of us actually trades—is attention, alongside the physical energy required to sustain it. We are gradually realizing that time is our only irreplaceable asset. Look around your space and you will see remarkably little that retains lasting value or demands replacing if used well. If anything, our possessions have become a burden: demanding an ongoing expenditure of attention while restricting our autonomy and sense of ease.

The reality of our social networks and their enablement of digital experiences is that we will need fewer durable goods—cars, refrigerators, televisions, and homes—along with the raw materials required to build them. Hedonic adaptation is a solved problem: you can wear rented Prada while reading digital Pravda. If that isn't a reconciliation of the Stoic and Epicurean impulses, I don't know what is.

This shift marks a fundamental departure from the industrial consumer engine of the twentieth century. Today's consumer society no longer depends on physical accumulation; instead, it latches onto the continuous digital marketing and harvesting of personal, private, and sensitive behavioral data for fun and profit. From media sensations like _13 Reasons Why_ to algorithmic influencers selling physical and ideological dross, attention itself has become the primary commodity.

When domestic populations transition from consuming heavy industrial goods to trading data and focus inside frictionless digital matrices, the traditional levers of U.S. power begin to slip. An empire built on military-industrial supply chains and the global enforcement of the petrodollar finds its traditional muscle neutered when the real theater of economic activity—and human desire—has migrated online.

Furthermore, whatever goods we do need can increasingly be manufactured by automated systems with greater precision and quality assurance—unburdened by expensive entitlements or middle management replaced by AI-enabled workflows.

Cultural conservatives aside, young people struggling to find the space, time, and money to self-actualize are increasingly foregoing children and long-term relationships altogether. They see little reason to invest in an economic machine led by an incumbent, geriatric generation seemingly intent on destroying their true inheritance: a livable planet.

The President is a product of his times, far too immersed in the daily firefight of retail politics in this age of digital marketing to truly reflect on a future where fewer things are bought and sold. Nor does he reckon with a reality in which the United States—representing just 4% of the world's population—can no longer weaponize its currency to dictate terms to the rest of the globe.

What becomes of soft-power influence—not just over countries, but over their intelligentsia, who no longer care about traditional forms of money, or for whom the accumulation of physical objects seems absurd? They do their work because it is inherently meaningful to them.

Furthermore, what remains of America’s moral differentiator to a Canadian or a Briton when Edward Snowden remains exiled in Moscow for highlighting that the U.S. Government and the NSA were breaking their own laws?

### Faustian Bargain

The United States finds itself in a particularly dicey position. While the rest of the world's productivity and society have been disciplined by the need to sell real things for money, the U.S. alone possessed what Charles de Gaulle termed the "extravagent privilege"—the ability to create as much reserve currency as it required. This was in any case a Faustian bargain. This privilege is becoming harder to maintain as new protocols denominate capital and move it seamlessly at a fraction of the cost of SWIFT and its counterparty-laden legacy alternatives—systems burdened by massive regulatory overhead to manage money laundering and terror financing risks.

Protocols like Ethereum and Solana are global and permissionless, allowing capital to move beyond the reach of traditional state sovereignty. Sovereign nations are answering with their own Digital Public Infrastructure, not as a rival creed but as a practical layer of identity that can meet those rails. India's Aadhaar is the clearest case so far: a civic mark of who a person is, able to sit beside open settlement without the protocol itself becoming a state.

The tissue is governance, not a platform war. Identity stays with the person. Settlement stays on the open protocol. Fraud and impersonation are checked at the seam between them. That is a philosophical claim about who holds the mark of a person, and a pragmatic one about how a community keeps its books.

Retail politicians exist to mollify public concerns on the street—often at the expense of transparency and basic fairness at home, and certainly overseas where state interests demand it. When election results are at stake, the priority is to shore up the nativist, blue-collar base, even if that entails coercing central bankers to prioritize political imperatives over monetary ones.

In Canada, much of the foundational work is carried out by civil servants—both traditional public servants and those in the private sector who view their work as delivering aligned value to humans with minimal ecological harm. Artificial intelligence excels at assessing the value of our communication, evaluating our online performance against standards of civic virtue. In effect, AI acts as an auditor, forcing us to close the open loops through which we emit cultural noise and ecological waste—negative externalities that impose a dead-weight loss on our communities and our planet. This is governance in the broad sense, and its future lies in protocols that automate transactional behavior around universally accepted norms written as ontology: the modern equivalent of basic law, hosted online and onchain.

As for those industrial jobs: wherever they were, and whether classified as blue-collar or white-collar, they are simply not coming back. Rather than looking back with nostalgia at the post-war consensus and the prosperity it brought, we must stay focused on how technology is evolving and how we can harness it to build a more optimistic vision—one where a natural order balances opposing forces. We are, collectively and individually, trendlines on a chart. The volatility of the present moment represents the separation of a multithreaded reality into its constituent identities: semantic perimeters of identity embodied, _semper idem_.

## Personality Disorder

Yet it is difficult to discipline an economy whose participants act simultaneously as consumers and citizens. Their atomized behaviors are not always aligned, and they seldom embody a single identity capable of balancing their present desires against their long-term self-interest. Instead, they are torn between individual cravings and community obligations—demanding that both be met immediately. On one hand, they seek low prices, quality, and hedonic variety; on the other, they demand abstract notions of diversity, equity, and inclusion everywhere.

The present challenge is that we are moving the authentication and validation of behavior online, away from physical driver's licenses and passports that represent sovereign state power. Simultaneously, we are transitioning from paper currency to digital monetary protocols. These can reflect community values as stablecoins or be deployed with frictionless ease by anyone—even as our society fractures over what constitutes truth, utility, and fairness, amidst a population increasingly indifferent to basic ethical constructs.

These digital protocols move between individuals with profound efficiency, making paper cash feel increasingly cumbersome. The use of paper cash for significant transactions is already treated as a red flag in places like Canada and Europe, where citizens are generally less suspicious of a government that, on the whole, has taken relatively good care of them compared to the American model.

Consider how the U.S. Dollar regime has played out since the Nixon Shock removed the dollar's peg to gold. The subsequent capacity to inject unlimited liquidity into the financial system financialized the broader economy, bifurcating it into "smart" and "dumb" money.

The United States maintains perhaps the world's most fiercely idealistic advocacy of human rights and democracy, on both the political right and left. Yet this idealism sits awkwardly alongside its government's imperial defense of the dollar regime. The next major shock came in 2008, when the Great Financial Crisis forced an unprecedented dilution of public wealth through TARP—disbursing over $440 billion in bailouts—which triggered populist activism across the entire political spectrum.

## Untenable Tenor

Our digital social networks will ultimately prove to be a boon—the space where we transact securely to assure lasting, sustainable prosperity. But the system remains incomplete; it is a parallel universe in its infancy, subject to chaotic collisions as we attract and repel one another in search of identity and community. Through these digital networks, we attempt to align and effect the change we all want: systemic integrity.

However, a sense of scarcity currently pervades our civilization. Its tenor is dictated across social networks largely by the dominant liberal democracy of the United States and its major technology conglomerates—including Meta, Google, Microsoft, Apple, and Amazon. While often labeled as tech conglomerates, platforms like Meta and Google are fundamentally media and communications firms that trade in attention. They transmute human focus into capital through advertising and algorithmic placement. In an era where software development itself is no longer scarce or technically formidable, the true battleground is no longer code—it is the orchestration of human focus.

On these platforms, revenue is driven by the quantity rather than the quality of debate, monetizing engineered engagement where algorithms and creators systematically rage-bait users into public conflict to harvest attention.

The result is a new Hobbesian war of all against all—an engine of manufactured friction frequently harnessed by institutions through digital marketing to score points against rival identities, whether individuals, communities, or sovereign states competing for credibility. In these turbulent times, true credibility—whether for an individual or a sovereign nation resisting low-intensity digital annexation—requires only one thing: consistency between one's private conduct and public demeanor, anchored by reason rather than algorithmic rage.

### Once The Dust Settles

The Red (or Civic) Ensign nicknamed the Duster was never originally conceived as a flag of military conquest; it was the banner of merchant mariners. It was the basis of Canada's original flag and remains the standard for several provinces including Alberta.

It represented the quiet, continuous flow of commerce—the lifeblood coursing through both the invisible hand of the market and the body politic of a sovereign nation. Long before the modern administrative state, it flew over a civilization anchored by a foundational commitment to peace, order, and good government rather than revolutionary zeal and overreach of empire. 

There is nothing so fundamentally anti-market as the arbitrary coercion of war, sanctions, and the digital subversion of another people, in a world where the end justifies meanness. Civility has always signaled both peace and prosperity.
