I rolled into the McDonald's to the resounding customary welcome—いらっしゃいませ! (irasshaimase). The restaurant was tucked into the shopping strip running along a generally bustling main street but one now quiet as the late grey sky shed a heavy rain. The road ran straight from my apartment down to Wakayama City's main arterial road at the base of which stood City Hall, the town's main hotel, and across the main arterial road, Wakayama Castle - a replica of the one which had stood there before the war.

I'm not sure now why I'd stepped out that afternoon. Alone in my apartment maybe I was just a little stir crazy and needed the air. My time in this provincial town was running down and in few months I'd be on the flight back to Toronto after two years. It was 1993 and I was twenty-three years old.

I'd just gotten into Norman Paterson - Carleton University's school of International Affairs. I was well set up. I'd stashed a ton of cash with Jardine Fleming the storied company of privateers that had become a Hong Kong based investment bank. If you've read James Clavell's swashbuckling Taipan and Noble House, that was them.

I had made bank. Gotten lucky as my stint in Japan coincided with the flow of capital into South East Asia. Me I was a kid who read about it in The Economist. It didn't take much back then to front run. You just had to read regularly. I had The Economist and The Guardian Weekly mailed to me in those days and read them cover to cover. 



Looking back now the world seemed so orderly and structured. I was still too outward focused to attend to whatever little there was in the way of baggage.

Teaching here had been a detour. Coming out my undergraduate program there was for the first time in my life there no obvious definition of progression. Today that kind of unbounded optionality was cause for excitement but back then the twenty-one year old version of me was unsteady and uncertain.

I was too burned to go straight to graduate school but the idea of taking any old job while decompressing, that too in the midst of a recession didn't really appeal to me. I wanted to do something *different* and considered joining the armed forces. I'd taken history and political science at Western and wanted an angle towards international affairs but my profile now needed more than a degree - even a formidable honors double major. 

At home for the winter break my dad handed me an ad he'd clipped out of the Toronto Star. The headline ran 'The Japan Exchange & Teaching Program'. It was run by the Japan's Ministry of Education. These were days when Japan was *all* the rage. Ezra Vogel, the Harvard sociologist had written 'Japan as Number One: Lessons for America'. Meanwhile the theme extended into the cinema with Rising Sun and Die Hard. The press meanwhile told of 'the Japanese' hoovering up trophy properties like Times Square and flooding North America with premium branded autos; their luxury marquees circumventing quotas with alacrity. Meanwhile T. Boone Pickens moaned about the country's barriers to foreign direct investment. Karel Van Wolferen wrote The Enigma of Japanese Power, a book essentially about what we would today call the Japanese Deep State

I applied and breezed through the application process and interviews; well before my final exams I was set, my course and destination locked in. 

These were the days before the internet. The fixed copper line in my home was attached to a rotary phone. The outside (English-speaking) world came at me through the BBC World Service on a Sony broadband radio and my subscriptions to the Guardian Weekly and the Economist. I had only just heard of the 'wonders' of email from one of my favourites, Anton Segal. Anton was a Harvard-trained anthropologist.

This, though, was a bustling provincial town at the very edges of global reality minding its own business. In some ways it reminded me of Baroda where I'd spent a year only a decade earlier. Baroda had its own elegant and modern districts and there was always plenty of good English copy to read. Still, it had the sub-continent's stark and raw poverty. Aside from one or two homeless folk camped out near the Wakayama-eki (the train station) you just didn't see that here—at least this far into the warmth of rustic reality far from cold metropolitan life.

Here in Wakayama City though I was famous; anyone who had a kid in junior high school knew exactly who I was. The city had sub-tropical charm reminiscent of the Mediterranean; tangerines and persimmon grew so plentifully that boxes of them were randomly left outside my door by kind neighbours whose children I taught in the city's junior high schools. However, even it had its local industrial champion—Noritsu—the photocopy and printing upstart and challenger to Xerox and Canon whose sprawling campus overlooked the city from a citadel higher than even the castle's at the northern edge of town. It looked for all the world like a Bond villain's lair, its posh Bauhaus lines looming from Wakayama's earthy homespun personality. The city was the capital of its eponymous prefecture—a gem missed by the casual tourist shuttling from Tokyo to Kobe via Kyoto and very few things in between.

Some of the most beautiful things I have ever seen are set upon Wakayama Prefecture's mountains. The most beautiful was Mount Kōya (高野山 — Kōyasan) with its complex of ancient Zen temples. I was fortunate to visit it as Asian skies brimmed with Pinatubo's ash. The skies and sunsets were spectacular that year and mountains layered on for you a reflected pastiche; a collage of riotous colours—purple, orange, red, pink and everything in between as light bounced between the suspended volcanic particles like inside a kaleidoscope.

But here I was now, bittersweet and coming to the end of my two-year stint as a teacher in the city's schools. I didn't know it then but I'd be back again—twice. For now though I was heading home to start grad school in Ottawa. I had just been accepted into Carleton's Norman Paterson School of International Affairs. My major was International Political Economy—a multi-disciplinary approach to understanding how power, influence and capital circulates among the institutions that bestride our planet to make our reality and our culture. I was keen to get back into the academic groove and the hard work of processing complex ideas about how our world was governed.

Now though, at the restaurant's counter I waited momentarily beyond which grills and fryers emitted their rich heavy and familiar fragrance. As I breathed it in, an enthusiastic employee rolled up to take my order then gestured me up the stairs to the second floor. I climbed up the narrow stairwell feeling my mood lift as I soaked in the warmth. I took a table by the full length glass front overlooking the grey street.


This was the beginning of *our* modernity; the era when we started to become the 'main character' of our paradigm; when it became obvious that we were every bit as well-endowed with biography and intellect—data and compute as we like to call it today—as any celebrity or so-called leader. Reality has become a simulation in eight billion processors endowed with personal brand.


The development of a *broader* consensus on the nature of objective value became an imperative: one which samples more broadly than a narrow subset of ever more monotonously bland views which seek above all to offend the fewest people as currency.


The wherewithal to create perfection in an espresso, a burger, a bicycle, or in the manufacture of a home which adds to the landscape is there for us all now. The artisan and his guild are cherished and longed for but we still live in a world which enjoys the familiar and ordinarily well-made: the licensed and banal familiarity of franchise marketed to a world which grows ever less comfortable with diversity and change.



## Even Better Than The Real Thing

When the world's biggest bands provide the soundtrack to your life and times. As you ride through life with high-fidelity sound adding just the right bespoke texture to what you see, hear, smell, touch, taste. This moment felt cinematic which is why I suppose it is still registered in my mind as something noteworthy.

I had U2's *Achtung Baby* running on my Sony Walkman; the device was scarcely larger than the cassette tape it held. It was however still descended and evolved after many iterations of engineering from the blue plastic classic I owned as a kid. I played the small machine in my right hand, turning it over and over. A stream of consciousness rolled through my mind to Akio Morita, Sony's founder and an abiding friendship with the conductor and composer Leonard Bernstein which I had come across somewhere. My tastes were more contemporary and low-brow than theirs—modern *English* music with its various strands of rhythm and melody anchored to philosophical and political commentary. Thinking of those men of culture and commerce, I wondered what might for them have constituted the cosmopolitan versus the communal; the parochial versus the all-encompassing and perennial. Their lives spanned the twentieth century with its heavy pain body of extinction, genocide and mass destruction; and now the lullaby of mass distraction.

As moderns with global vision, mercantile though they were, they still had to operate in the parochial realities of tribal loyalty. I expect each was pulled into choosing between the solidarity regimented by His Master's Voice or perhaps the Japan Victor Company on the one hand, and solitude of the *masterless*—the anarchist and ronin on the other. My mind now ran to George Michael, his conflict with Sony Records over artistic freedom, his track *Freedom '90* with its essential but borrowed sample of James Brown's Funky Drummer. It was a short hop then to Sinéad O'Connor, her audacious protest against the Catholic patriarchy on *SNL* just a few weeks ago, and her own sampling of the Funky Drummer on *I Am Stretched on Your Grave.* We are all performers, composers, entrepreneurs. We are all endeavouring to manufacture something original, stamping intellectual authenticity—even as we stand on the shoulders of giants—our common intellectual heritage: the things which elevate and enrich us without impoverishing others.

Here now arrives up the stairs my good man carrying a tray of food. He sets it down in front of me, steps back and bows deeply, almost reverentially, so that I'm taken aback. Suddenly I'm emotional and already imagining how I will reminisce about this land and its people. The good times I have had and the friends made. The food—a Combo (Big Mac, large fries and a Coke)—the very symbol of modern commercialism is piping hot and delicious. Here I am suspended between—on one hand—the obvious practicality and cheap, abundant comforts of familiar fast food, nearly a taste of home, far across the ocean; and on the other, the plain fact that I remain a stranger in a strange land: a wayfarer without a master—a ronin.

浪人 (*ronin*) is made of two characters meaning wave and person. It denotes a masterless samurai bereft of a liege and made self-sovereign by necessity rather than design. My own heritage lends additional nuance. *Ro* phonetically resembles the Gujarati word for “stay” (રહો), while *nin*, “person” in Japanese, reads in English like the self—the “I”—hemmed in on either side by negation: the none, the nil, the nothing in particular that nevertheless demands allegiance.

The character itself suggests a makeshift dwelling—a tent, a hearth—belonging implied but unsecured. Or perhaps it is simply a *Y* inverted. The masterless samurai stands upright, the wheels turning, yet his sense of purpose is upside down. His deepest question is not *how* but *why*. The anarchist in me looks both ways and insists on a positive sum: a reason to support something, rather than merely another excuse to reject everything.

Models are frameworks for understanding the world. They are not judged on verbatim high fidelity but on their capacity to yield insight as a lens. The question is whether our visualisations lean toward meaning—or are they a mirage? A hallucination like a siren beckoning us into the shimmer and shine of an imaginary oasis? This is the space where the boundaries that delineate art (beauty) and science (truth) blur or come into focus as either futility or utility: where wheels spin to go nowhere, or where one makes a stand only to find that *stillness* yields value. Some lenses are difficult. They force you to acknowledge the insanity of the broadly accepted model of reality and march instead, like George Michael, Sinéad O'Connor—or for that matter Public Enemy—to the base and beat of the *Funky* Drummer.
