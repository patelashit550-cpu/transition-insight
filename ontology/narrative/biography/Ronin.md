## Kii Peninsula

I rolled into the McDonald's to the resounding customary welcome—いらっしゃいませ! (_Irasshaimase!_). The restaurant was tucked into the shopping strip running along a generally bustling pedestrian mall, but one now quiet as a grey evening sky shed its heavy rain. The road ran straight from my apartment down to Wakayama City's main arterial road, at the base of which stood City Hall, the town's main (three-star) hotel. Beyond them was the city's arterial spine the highway which ran through it and then down into the lush sub-tropical countryside of Wakayama prefecture. On the other side of that avenue rose Wakayama Castle—a replica of the one which  stood there before the war and which once housed branches of the elite Tokugawa Shogunate.
  
I'm not sure now why I'd stepped out that afternoon. Alone in my apartment, I was probably just a little stir-crazy and needed some air. My time in this provincial town an hour south of Osaka on the coast of the Kii Peninsula was running down, and in a few months I'd be on a flight back to Toronto after having spent two years teaching in the city's junior high schools. I was twenty-three years old in this late winter of 1993.

I'd just gotten into Norman Paterson, Carleton University's School of International Affairs in Ottawa. The wind was at my back again and I that confident air again; of someone whose path, a moment ago uncertain, was once again laid before him. This was where Canada's expatriate non-governmental, corporate and diplomatic corps immersed themselves in the study of political-economy, conflict and development in the global context; where they sought to understand  ideas concerning loyalties to arbitrary concepts of nationality, faith, and tribalism in all its forms and how they impacted upon prosperity and standing in an increasingly complex and transnational global reality. A year from now I would be submerged underneath a torrent of reading—technical journals—not to mention examinations and writing and the competitive parry and riposte of debating with self-styled Marxists in class. I would become the class bête noire; broadly typecast (in my view badly) as a right-wing libertarian with connections to India's new (Fascist) government. Mostly I think just because I was brown, pro-markets and spent time in Japan (rather than say Senegal).

The deflation felt just days earlier was lifted. A backup plan fell through when the London School of Economics turned me down; I was bracing myself for further disappointment; induction into Norman Patterson was every bit as hotly contested after all even if its cache was mostly domestic.

On the other hand I had options with which to continue my procrastination and delay the inevitable entry into the next stage of life; I wasn't ready to commit to a career. The world wide web hadn't arrived and the dotcom boom was still years away. We were still in a time when you really could disappear on the other side of the world and that was exactly what I'd done. 

Here in Japan I only had the fixed line of my rotary phone connecting me to the world. Otherwise there was only Sony short-wave transistor radio and two subscriptions (*The Guardian Weekly* and *The Economist*) connecting me to the world at large. I also still spent a lot of time writing letters to friends and family back home. Beyond this of course were my community of friends both expatriate, largely American, Australian, British and Canadian—and as well of course—Japanese. 

All in all it this had been a time of decompression, introspection and reflection. However I had no clear sense of what I wanted to do for the rest of my life.

So far the University of Essex's program in Japanese Studies in Colchester, as well as Simon Fraser's graduate program in Political Science were my two options. I was hungry again for intellectual growth, having worked off my undergraduate burnout in this quietly bustling provincial town. It had served as a hub as I reached into the most beautiful places both right here in the prefecture and beyond throughout Kansai—as well as South Korea, Thailand, and Malaysia. However for me  nothing was ever so beautiful, elegant and tasteful as Wakayama-ken and more broadly Kansai and with all its bristling cultural breadth.

Certainly it was particularly scenic in that moment. Mount Pinatubo's 1991 eruption had filled East Asia's skies with particulate which reflected in kaleidoscopic glory. The sunsets were always glorious everywhere. However in all my years of travelling nothing I had ever seen was ever so beautiful as Koyasan's complex of austere monasticism perched high in the mountains. Negotiating the hairpins on the way down we stopped for a while at a motoring lookout point and my eyes welled up as I gazed out at an assorted range of pastel layered pink and purples mountain ranges. I was emotionally overcome by the awesome sight.

## Man In Society

Now though I was going back to the theoretical and data-oriented world of academia. I worked hard at school. I loved the books and threw myself into study. I even looked back at High School and thought wow we get school for free. What a deal—what a privilege. What a thing we might make of that opportunity. Or not. Even then I imagined what the world would look like if its data might tune one precisely into becoming perfectly aligned with our innate abilities so that our array of potentiality might be lived with greater fulfillment in society. We would be drawn inexorably to calling with an auto-didactic inexorability.  

School takes its toll on you though when you take it seriously; but I supposed everything does when that's the case. Hard mental work however steels you when you persevere through it. As though you were being folded, pleated and forged like the sharp edge of a tool, God only knows to what end. 

My work here in Japan was generally a lot of fun but there were long dull spells. It was. The model required us to *team teach* with Japanese teachers of English. Most of my expatriate friends were assigned a single school in some rural district whereas I made my way through eighteen different schools spending two weeks at each. A situation which made it difficult to hit a routine involving consistent structured planning. I became more of an ambassador making myself available to the wide-eyed youngsters many of whom had never spoken to a foreigner.

Planning lessons Physically I was in the shape of my life. I spent some two hours at least whizzing through the town on my mountain bike, trained with weights gym and practiced Karate, securing a brown belt in Japan. One more year and I might have come home with a black.

I was also flush with cash. I'd worked summers all the way through my undergraduate years at the Richmond Hill Country Club. At first, washing dishes and bussing tables, then as a waiter, bartender, and a bouncer at the club's (mostly Jewish) wedding parties. I'd accumulated very little in the way of debt by the time I graduated in the summer of 1991. Then, barely a month or two after graduating, I was on a flight bound for Japan, getting oriented alongside hundreds of international, bright young things from across the English-speaking West at Tokyo's swank Keio Plaza Hotel before setting out across the country to take up our posts.

My job with Monbusho (Japan's Ministry of Education) paid a startling  of $45,000 year—a lot of money for twenty-one year old in 1991. Over the past two years I had also ridden the wave of foreign direct investment into South East Asia; investing my savings into Jardine Fleming's emerging market funds. That was still rather exotic thing to do for a suburban middle-class kid—not at all the normie mainstream fare. The return was stellar. 

To be honest I was every bit as taken with Jardine Fleming's swashbuckling and undoubtedly embellished history as told by James Clavell in his *Taipan* and *Noble House* which I devoured in pretty much a single sitting over a winter weekend while bingeing on mikan (tangerines), ramen and mugi-cha (roasted barley tea). I read a lot in those days, devoured page-turners offering an easy escapism when the solitude of being away from the familiar environs of home became overwhelming. This wasn't Tokyo, Osaka or Kyoto and I stood out. I was a particularly rare variant of *Western.

I similarly drove right through Douglas Adams' *Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy* as well, while Joe Jackson's Greatest Hits played over and over on my stereo, now forever bound to that brilliant and hilarious series. 




At home for the winter break my dad handed me an ad he'd clipped out of the Toronto Star. The headline ran 'The Japan Exchange & Teaching Program'. It was run by the Japan's Ministry of Education. These were days when Japan was *all* the rage. Ezra Vogel, the Harvard sociologist had written 'Japan as Number One: Lessons for America'. Meanwhile the theme extended into the cinema with Rising Sun and Die Hard. The press meanwhile told of 'the Japanese' hoovering up trophy properties like Times Square and flooding North America with premium branded autos; their luxury marquees circumventing quotas with alacrity. Meanwhile T. Boone Pickens moaned about the country's barriers to foreign direct investment. Karel Van Wolferen wrote The Enigma of Japanese Power, a book essentially about what we would today call the Japanese Deep State

I applied and breezed through the application process and interviews; well before my final exams I was set, my course and destination locked in. 







But here I was now, bittersweet and coming to the end of my two-year stint as a teacher in the city's schools. I didn't know it then but I'd be back again—twice. For now though I was heading home to start grad school in Ottawa. I had just been accepted into Carleton's Norman Paterson School of International Affairs. My major was International Political Economy—a multi-disciplinary approach to understanding how power, influence and capital circulates among the institutions that bestride our planet to make our reality and our culture. I was keen to get back into the academic groove and the hard work of processing complex ideas about how our world was governed.

Now though, at the restaurant's counter I waited momentarily beyond which grills and fryers emitted their rich heavy and familiar fragrance. As I breathed it in, an enthusiastic employee rolled up to take my order then gestured me up the stairs to the second floor. I climbed up the narrow stairwell feeling my mood lift as I soaked in the warmth. I took a table by the full length glass front overlooking the grey street.


This was the beginning of *our* modernity; the era when we started to become the 'main character' of our paradigm; when it became obvious that we were every bit as well-endowed with biography and intellect—data and compute as we like to call it today—as any celebrity or so-called leader. Reality has become a simulation in eight billion processors endowed with personal brand.


The development of a *broader* consensus on the nature of objective value became an imperative: one which samples more broadly than a narrow subset of ever more monotonously bland views which seek above all to offend the fewest people as currency.


The wherewithal to create perfection in an espresso, a burger, a bicycle, or in the manufacture of a home which adds to the landscape is there for us all now. The artisan and his guild are cherished and longed for but we still live in a world which enjoys the familiar and ordinarily well-made: the licensed and banal familiarity of franchise marketed to a world which grows ever less comfortable with diversity and change.



## Even Better Than The Real Thing

When the world's biggest bands provide the soundtrack to your life and times. As you ride through life with high-fidelity sound adding just the right bespoke texture to what you see, hear, smell, touch, taste. This moment felt cinematic which is why I suppose it is still registered in my mind as something noteworthy.

I had U2's *Achtung Baby* running on my Sony Walkman; the device was scarcely larger than the cassette tape it held. It was however still descended and evolved after many iterations of engineering from the blue plastic classic I owned as a kid. I played the small machine in my right hand, turning it over and over. A stream of consciousness rolled through my mind to Akio Morita, Sony's founder and an abiding friendship with the conductor and composer Leonard Bernstein which I had come across somewhere. My tastes were more contemporary and low-brow than theirs—modern *English* music with its various strands of rhythm and melody anchored to philosophical and political commentary. Thinking of those men of culture and commerce, I wondered what might for them have constituted the cosmopolitan versus the communal; the parochial versus the all-encompassing and perennial. Their lives spanned the twentieth century with its heavy pain body of extinction, genocide and mass destruction; and now the lullaby of mass distraction.

As moderns with global vision, mercantile though they were, they still had to operate in the parochial realities of tribal loyalty. I expect each was pulled into choosing between the solidarity regimented by His Master's Voice or perhaps the Japan Victor Company on the one hand, and solitude of the *masterless*—the anarchist and ronin on the other. My mind now ran to George Michael, his conflict with Sony Records over artistic freedom, his track *Freedom '90* with its essential but borrowed sample of James Brown's Funky Drummer. It was a short hop then to Sinéad O'Connor, her audacious protest against the Catholic patriarchy on *SNL* just a few weeks ago, and her own sampling of the Funky Drummer on *I Am Stretched on Your Grave.* We are all performers, composers, entrepreneurs. We are all endeavouring to manufacture something original, stamping intellectual authenticity—even as we stand on the shoulders of giants—our common intellectual heritage: the things which elevate and enrich us without impoverishing others.

Here now arrives up the stairs my good man carrying a tray of food. He sets it down in front of me, steps back and bows deeply, almost reverentially, so that I'm taken aback. Suddenly I'm emotional and already imagining how I will reminisce about this land and its people. The good times I have had and the friends made. The food—a Combo (Big Mac, large fries and a Coke)—the very symbol of modern commercialism is piping hot and delicious. Here I am suspended between—on one hand—the obvious practicality and cheap, abundant comforts of familiar fast food, nearly a taste of home, far across the ocean; and on the other, the plain fact that I remain a stranger in a strange land: a wayfarer without a master—a ronin.



## Ronin

浪人 (*ronin*) is made of two characters meaning wave and person. It denotes a masterless samurai bereft of a liege and made self-sovereign by necessity rather than design. My own heritage lends additional nuance. *Ro* phonetically resembles the Gujarati word for “stay” (રહો), while *nin*, “person” in Japanese, reads in English like the self—the “I”—hemmed in on either side by negation: the none, the nil, the nothing in particular that nevertheless demands allegiance.

The character itself suggests a makeshift dwelling—a tent, a hearth—belonging implied but unsecured. Or perhaps it is simply a *Y* inverted. The masterless samurai stands upright, the wheels turning, yet his sense of purpose is upside down. His deepest question is not *how* but *why*. The anarchist in me looks both ways and insists on a positive sum: a reason to support something, rather than merely another excuse to reject everything.

Models are frameworks for understanding the world. They are not judged on verbatim high fidelity but on their capacity to yield insight as a lens. The question is whether our visualisations lean toward meaning—or are they a mirage? A hallucination like a siren beckoning us into the shimmer and shine of an imaginary oasis? This is the space where the boundaries that delineate art (beauty) and science (truth) blur or come into focus as either futility or utility: where wheels spin to go nowhere, or where one makes a stand only to find that *stillness* yields value. Some lenses are difficult. They force you to acknowledge the insanity of the broadly accepted model of reality and march instead, like George Michael, Sinéad O'Connor—or for that matter Public Enemy—to the base and beat of the *Funky* Drummer.
