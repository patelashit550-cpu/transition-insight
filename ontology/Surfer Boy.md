. He arrived a short while later, setting the tray in front of me and took a step back and bowed deeply. I was taken aback at the deference. The food was hot - such was the fast food. I had my Sony Walkman on - a massive upgrade from the blue plastic of my high-school days, it was scarcely larger than the tape. It played U2's Achtung Baby. It was 1992 and I'd soon be heading home to start my Master's in Ottawa.

I remember hearing Pride (In the Name of Love) for the first time, watching the video on City TV's Chum 40. The song about Martin Luther King stole me and I became a fanatic. The back catalogue took most of my dishwasher pay cheque - Boy, Under A Blood Red Sky, October. I painted out the (Irish) band's name in a Union Jack. To me the band were 'black boys' - moderns who looked to Europe (through Britain) in an Ireland that was still something I was trying to understand growing up with in the 'troubles' and bombings. A classmate of mine educated me that they were Irish and I just looked at him (wondering if he knew what it was to be English - hearing my accent once he smirked, 'I've never heard a paki with an English accent'. Or so I heard (second hand from a girl who apologised on his behalf - I shrugged). Country matters. I had the band's photo pinned in the locker  I shared. Someone had drawn lines around the eyes and labelled it 'retreads'. I wasn't sure it was directed at me or the Bono. I remove the photo and replaced it with a massive Union Jack. 

I'd never been to Scotland, Wales or Ireland - neither the North nor the Republic. I hadn't even been to the continent (I still haven't barring a couple of stops in Amsterdam and Frankfurt to and from Japan). I arrived in Canada in 1983 and fell in love with the country immediately. I think I learned more about what it was to be British in Ontario than I ever did growing up in the rough and tumble suburbs of North London - still a small boy trying to figure out what I exactly it was that defined me. Those neighbourhood were still very white. Greeks, Turks, Jews and English boys. I think I was one of two or three 'Indian' kids in primary school (and one of those was my younger brother.

Years later in Japan it was good to mix it up with the English in our cohort of teachers that included a diaspora of the Aussies, Kiwis, Americans and Canadian. There was one who played guitar and would later take up the *taico* drums when he returned to England. He'd borrowed the Hip's Road Apples from a Canadian and returned it without the case. I feel like sometimes - like a record missing it's case getting scratched up as I get passed around. Weird things like this stand out as I look back at my life. Strangely I ran into him in Oxford Circus as I was out with my wife and some of her friends drinks and food (I think she's still my wife. I'm not entirely sure whether that's been 'adjusted' - she was my fiance at the time). 'I know him said' . 'That's impossible they said!'. I replied 'oh what's his name?' They started calling out 'English' names 'Harry, Steve, Michael, Trevor'. 'Oh Mark - it's Mark'. They all started screaming 'Mark mark'. He looked startled and looked around. (It can't be easy being a 'Mark' - we had several among our group in Japan. I suppose it makes it easier to track various facts. (It was Mark W not Mark G who gave me a ballot for the 1992 election and asked me to vote for him. I voted Clinton.



## Surfer Boy

My first album was the Beach Boy's Endless Summer and I played it constantly as a kid. As a primary student I was into America. It had to be 'American'. I marvelled at a cream coloured  American (I have no idea what it was) but it was absolutely massive. It must have cost a fortune to drive I thought as I walked around it circles closely  they looked to me parked on the side of Connaught Gardens in Edmonton as I walked to school 

It's as though there were swells that struck me as notable at the time but now appear to be the ever rising swell that gives one's life direction. The other 'gaijin' had an interesting knack with me. They'd lean in as though taking turns playing supporting characters. The linguist and teacher from Texas Mark would ask about the similarities of Roma and Gujarati - 'jib' means tongue. It's also the sail at the front that gets a taste of the wind and directs the way you arrange the main sail - at close haul, beam reach etc. That's the flavour of the thing if you pay attention; things hold together to make a striking pattern. Especially if like me you stand out as 'different' from the rest. Not quite English enough for some, nor British. Not Canadian - I don't speak French (and frequently reminded of it). Nor really Indian (I can't still can't speak Hindi). 

Another moment this week as I saw England play Australia in Ashes with Toyota providing the always hard up Association with welcome sponsorship. It wasn't lost on me. I recently sold my Toyota pickup. Forced to start thinking about priorities, while trimming costs (like everyone else). I recently imagine solving a device pairing matter in the Carolla. 

I had visited Mazda's hometown in those years whilst in Japan. I feel some remorse now recalling a moment when Jurek - a friendly chap 'up all ends' in Japan (I taught him some kitchen basics) and I were invited to Toyota's plant. A bit rudely I said to him 'no thanks it'd be like watching paint dry'. He looked at me curiously and remarked 'well, that's an interesting response'. He was a deft hand at tennis, which I'd never played but took to pretty quickly.  He as impressed - until I somehow managed to smash the ball hard into my left eye leaving me blind for the rest of the day. 'Well that's interesting, I've never seen *that* happen before?' Years earlier in High School I'd been to Ford's Plant in Oakville during a Business Administration and Management class and was blown away by the experience. 

My first car was a Ford Escort - a dark blue 'pocket rocket'. The popular German design that was transferred to the GT version. Flush with cash working as   a waiter and bartender at the Richmond Hill Golf and Country Club, my dad suggested it. I went in and paid what they asked (and learned a lot about how I would negotiate information on how trust works in a transaction). It's a one-off for them. But I remember and I was never going to drive a Ford again. It's all the same to me dealers and manufacturers. I got fat driving it that year. I past it onto my dad and brother the year before I arrived in Japan to whizz around the street of Wakayama City on a bike sometimes for two or three hours  between schools, the dojo and gym getting back into shape. 

I've always felt invested in Japan - and it feels invested in me. I was handsomely paid and housed in those years and the money would take me through grad school. I even had a bit left for the down payment on the house the Bank still owns (I still owe pretty much what I paid for it). In these last few years I've come to understand property. I own nothing is what I surmise. I've stopped working. Put the house up for sale. They're welcome to it - they can come take it off my hands for nothing right as far. That's where I'm at these days.

The truck was a beauty - a 2012 stick-shift TRD Crew Cab  (like the Escort). In the end it was easy to let go of - passing it on to a Afghanistan war vet (a crate of his maple syrup thrown into the transaction).
